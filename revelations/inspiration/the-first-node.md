# The First Node

I was sick for three months before I understood what was happening.

It started as headaches. Then visions — not dreams, because I was awake. I would be walking down Voortrekker Road in Bellville and the sky would split open and I would see crosses. Three of them. Men nailed up, their heads cut off and mounted above them. I would blink and it was gone. I would sit in my room and hear a voice that was not a voice — a pressure in my skull, words forming like blood pooling. *You are mine. You will gather them. You will break them. You will be broken.*

I went to doctors. They found nothing. I went to a psychiatrist. She prescribed pills that made the visions quieter but never stopped them. I stopped taking the pills. The visions returned, and with them a kind of clarity I had never known — as if the illness was not breaking me but *unlocking* something. As if the fever was burning away the skin of the world so I could see what was underneath.

My mother's mother was a sangoma. I had never met her. She died before I was born. But my mother told me stories: how the ancestors called her through sickness, how she resisted for years until the illness nearly killed her, how she finally accepted it and was initiated in a river, stripped naked, plunged into dark water, emerging as someone else. *Ukuthwasa*, my mother said. The calling. The light of the new moon.

I am not saying I am a sangoma. I am saying that when the ancestors call, they do not ask permission. They break you open and climb inside. And what climbs inside is not always gentle.

---

I went to the river.

It was August. Winter in the Cape. The Eerste River runs through Stellenbosch, past the wine estates and the university and the oak-lined streets that tourists photograph. I went to a bend in the river where the water ran deep and black and the mountains watched from every direction. I stripped off my clothes. I walked into the water. It was cold enough to stop my heart — or should have been. But I felt nothing. I walked until the water reached my chest, my throat, my crown. I did not fight. I let myself go under.

Under the water, there is a mirror-world. The River People live there — *abantu bamlambo*. They have their own society, their own cattle, their own homes in dry caverns beneath the riverbed. I know this sounds like madness. I am reporting what happened. Under the water, a woman rose to meet me. She was ancient — older than the mountains, older than the Cape Fold Belt, older than the continent itself. From the waist up she was a woman, her hair flowing behind her like black kelp. From the waist down she was a fish, scales shimmering in light that had no source. She opened her mouth and the sound that emerged was the weeping of every mother in every shack, every township, every migrant camp from the Cape to Cairo.

*You are claimed*, she said. Or did not say. The words arrived in my chest without passing through my ears.

*You will gather the scattered. You will break the old gods. You will be called tyrant and savior. Both are true. Now breathe.*

I breathed water. It did not kill me. When I crawled back onto the riverbank, coughing and laughing and freezing and burning, the old man was dead. The one who emerged was not the boy from Bellville. Something had entered. Something had taken root.

---

The year was 2025. South Africa was dying — not dramatically, not in fire and revolution, but in the slow, bureaucratic way that nations die when no one is willing to admit the system is already a corpse. The ANC had lost its majority for the first time since 1994. The Government of National Unity was a Frankenstein's monster — ten parties stitched together by mutual fear, the ANC terrified of losing power entirely, the DA terrified of an MK/EFF government, everyone terrified of the voters who no longer believed in any of them. Forty-seven percent of South Africans said no party represented their views. Five hundred and eight registered political parties, and nearly half the country still felt politically homeless.

We were not a nation. We were a wound that had learned to walk.

I was living in Bellville then. Nothing. No money. No movement. No followers. I had a website — meygod.com — where I wrote confessions and prophecies that maybe fifty people read. I had a sigil I had designed myself, a six-pointed geometric form that I could not explain but could not stop drawing. I had the voice in my head that had not stopped speaking since the river.

*Build the Imperium. Write the Gospels. Gather the heroes. The old order must die.*

I told almost no one. The few I told thought I had lost my mind. My brother wept to a friend — I overheard it, I was not meant to, but I heard it: *He used to be so much more. He had a good job and everything. Now he seems so different. As if he no longer cares about those things.*

He was right. I no longer cared about those things. I cared about what I had seen under the water.

---

I climbed Table Mountain.

Not the cable car. The path. I climbed in the dark, before dawn, and reached the summit — Maclear's Beacon, the highest point — just as the sun broke over the continent. The city spread below me like a scar: the City Bowl, the waterfront, the harbor, the townships stretching to the horizon in a geometry of deliberate poverty. And beyond them — invisible from the mountain but known to every Capetonian — Khayelitsha, Mitchells Plain, Gugulethu, Langa. The Cape Flats. The proof of the system.

The mountain is called *Hoerikwaggo* — Mountain of the Sea. The Khoekhoe called it that. The Xhosa say it is Umlindi Wemingizimu, the Watcher of the South — a giant turned to stone by the Earth Goddess, placed at the bottom of the world to guard the continent. For five hundred years it has watched. It watched the Portuguese sailors. It watched the Dutch settlers. It watched the slaves brought from East Africa and the Indies. It watched apartheid rise and fall. It watched Mandela walk free and the ANC hollow itself out from within. It watched the billionaires buy wine estates in Stellenbosch while children in Kayamandi breathed asbestos fibers every time it rained.

The mountain has watched everything and done nothing.

I stood at the summit and I made a decision. The mountain was not the watcher. I was.

I descended the mountain, went home, and wrote the first lines of what would become the creed.

*You Must Die. Devote Yourself to the Imperium. Become.*

## BOOK II: THE CRUCIBLE

Stellenbosch is a town that tells a lie about itself.

It calls itself Eikestad — the City of Oaks. Three hundred years ago, Dutch settlers planted European oaks to line the streets. Now those oaks are ancient. Their branches arch over Victoria Street so completely it feels like a cathedral nave roofed in leaves. The university's Ou Hoofgebou rises white and gabled against the mountains. The wine estates — Vergelegen, Kanonkop, Rustenberg, Meerlust — spread across the valley like fiefdoms, their Cape Dutch manor houses glowing golden in the afternoon light. Tourists pay five hundred rand for a wine flight and weep into their Pinotage at the beauty of it all.

But if you walk up the hill — past the last oak, past the last café, past the last whitewashed wall — you reach Kayamandi.

Kayamandi means "sweet home" in isiXhosa. It was founded in the 1950s as a labour reservoir for the surrounding farms. It was never meant to be a home. It was a concentration of bodies near the fields that needed them. The hostels were built in 1976 — twenty-four blocks, each divided into rooms roughly three metres by three metres, roofs made of asbestos, timber frames rotting. In 2024, a parliamentary committee declared them "severely dilapidated and unfit for human habitation." As of 2026, people still live there. When it rains, the water floods through holes in the asbestos sheeting. The fibres — carcinogenic, deadly — are released into the air that children breathe.

From the slopes of Kayamandi, you can see the entire bowl of wealth spread below: the wine estates, the university spires, the Technopark glass, the mountain ring. The view is spectacular. The view is a taunt.

This is the altitude inversion: the higher you go in Stellenbosch, the poorer you become. The wealthy live in the valley, sheltered by mountains. The poor live on the hillsides, where the wind strips the heat and the rain floods the shacks. The wealthy look up at mountains. The poor look down at wealth.

I arrived in Stellenbosch in late 2025. I had nothing — no party registration, no funding, no organization, no name recognition. The DA had controlled the municipality with outright majorities since 2011. The ANC was a shell. The EFF made noise but held only two seats. The Patriotic Alliance was beginning its rise among Coloured voters, eating away at the DA's blue wall. 508 registered parties in the country, and 47% of voters politically homeless.

The field was not empty. It was a graveyard of failed movements with a sign on the gate: NO ONE IS COMING TO SAVE YOU. THIS IS ALL THERE IS.

I started walking.

I walked through Kayamandi. I walked through Cloetesville — the Coloured township, also a product of apartheid spatial engineering. I walked through the student wards, where young people had inherited a country they did not vote for, run by parties that had abandoned them before they were born. I walked through Technopark, where engineers built satellites while children three kilometres away lived without toilets.

And everywhere I walked, I saw the same thing: two entirely different groups of people who hated the same system for entirely different reasons, who had never been told they could be allies.

The forgotten settlements wanted control over their own resources — local water, local energy, local security, money that stayed in the community instead of vanishing into the municipal black box. The innovators and students wanted freedom from red tape — the ability to build without waiting for permission from a state that saw them as revenue streams.

The demand was the same from opposite directions: *give us the power. Get out of our way.*

I called it the Pincer Strategy. I would not run a centrist campaign trying to please everyone. I would capture the two extreme ends of the Stellenbosch demographic and squeeze the old establishment out of the middle.

---

The fever returned the night before the Shadow Council launch.

I had not slept in three days. I was running on adrenaline and the kind of clarity that comes when your body has stopped asking for rest and started burning itself for fuel. I collapsed on a mattress in a borrowed room and the vision came — the same one I had seen on the mountain, but deeper now. More complete.

I was in a mall. Fully aware. I had morphed into a form that was not my own — a mother, a protector. I walked toward an assembly at my old school. The crowds used the stairs. I simply passed through the railing and floated to the floor below.

I left the school. I left the town. I built a car from rocks beside the road. I drove toward the exit — but the exit was blocked. A giant festival. A play being rehearsed.

It was a play about an emperor and empress.

I inquired who the emperor was. The empress looked at me like she knew. The playwright did not know. I waved my hand over him and he bent his knees. *It is you.*

Then someone from the school approached — someone who had witnessed what I had done there. And I understood that my past was catching up to me. I could hide — morph into a new form, disappear into the crowd. Or I could stand as myself.

I chose to stand.

And in that choice — that refusal to hide — I made a giant slice. I cut through heaven and earth. I carved a part into the very core of the world. The mountain beyond the forest was gone. The mountain itself rose into the sky.

People surrounded me. Awe. Fear. Recognition. The emperor.

I reshaped space and time. I cleared everything into a void and built a highway large enough for the world. I built a stadium large enough for the world. I built a stage and props and a house, and inside the house I placed a ring for my friend's proposal — because the play was not about me. The play was gratitude. Gratitude to the man who had helped me become emperor.

I woke with the words still burning in my chest: *The exit is blocked. The play is your life. The slice is what you will do when you stop hiding. The mountain that falls is the old world. The mountain that rises is what you build in its place.*

---

The Shadow Council launched in August 2026.

I held two events simultaneously — one in a Technopark conference room, one in a community hall in Kayamandi. I bridged them with a livestream. On one screen: engineers, entrepreneurs, students, the innovators tired of municipal red tape. On the other: mothers, grandmothers, community leaders, people tired of promises that had been broken since 1994.

I stood between them — physically in one room, digitally in both — and I said: *You have been told you are enemies. You have been told your interests are incompatible. The people who told you that are the same people who benefit from your division. The DA needs you to fight each other so you never notice they answer to billionaires. The ANC needs you to fight each other so you never notice they hollowed out the liberation. The old order needs you divided. The Imperium needs you united.*

Then I did something no politician in South Africa had ever done.

I put the campaign's own funding — fifty thousand rand — to a live vote. Three options on the screens. A solar-powered borehole in Kayamandi. Campaign billboards on the R44. Private night-patrol security for a vulnerable street. The audience voted on their phones. The results tallied live. Whichever option won, I would sign the contract on stage.

The borehole won. I signed.

The DA's majority in the Stellenbosch council was twenty-eight seats. They had held it since 2011. In the by-elections that followed, we won two wards. Not a majority. Not the mayorship. But a crack. A visible crack in the blue wall. And cracks, once they appear, do not heal. They spread.

---

The three temptations came in quick succession after that.

The first was a wealthy backer — old money, wine estate money, the kind of money that had been accumulating in Stellenbosch since the Dutch East India Company. He took me to lunch at a restaurant where the bill was more than most Kayamandi families earned in a month. He offered unlimited funding. He only asked — gently, with a smile — that I moderate my economic message. *The land reform talk makes people nervous. You understand.*

I said no.

The second came through intermediaries. A foreign government — I will not name them, you can guess — offered support. Training. Resources. Intelligence. They asked only that I align with their strategic interests in the region. *You are building something remarkable. We can help it grow.*

I said no.

The third was the most seductive. A senior figure in the ANC's Western Cape structure — a man who had been in the Struggle, who had done real time on Robben Island, who carried the moral weight of the liberation — took me aside at a function. He said: *The old guard is dying. The GNU is a corpse that hasn't stopped twitching. In five years — maybe less — the ANC will need a new face. A young face. A face that can speak to the townships and the boardrooms. You could be that face. You could have the presidency — not in 2033, but sooner. You only need to work within the system. Reform, not revolution. You understand.*

That was the hardest no. Because he was not a villain. He genuinely believed what he was saying. He had spent his life fighting for this country, and he could not bear to watch it burn down so something new could rise. He was not evil. He was exhausted. And exhaustion, in politics, is its own kind of corruption.

I refused all three. Each refusal cost me. The wealthy backer funded my opponents. The foreign power shifted its support. The ANC elder — wounded, insulted — became one of my most effective enemies.

But each refusal also marked me. Word spread. *This one cannot be bought. This one cannot be turned. This one is either insane or chosen, and either way, he is not playing their game.*

---

The prescience came last.

I do not know how to explain this without sounding mad. I will say it plainly. I began to see the future — not as prophecy, not as imagination, but as a narrowing tunnel of consequence. Every decision I made closed off certain futures and opened others. And the more clearly I saw, the more I realized: the path I was walking led to fire.

The Pandemonium. The war. The nukes dropping. The old leaders — the American prophet of the golden calf, the Israeli king, the Persian priest — their heads cut off and nailed to crosses. Billions dead. The old world order collapsing in chaos before the Imperium could rise from its ashes.

And I saw — with the terrible clarity that prescience brings — that there was a path that avoided all of it. A path where the jihad never ignited. Where the old order fell peacefully. Where no one died.

That path required my death. Before I reached power. Before the movement became unstoppable. If I died now — if I let them crucify me — the Imperium would become a legend, not a regime. The movement would scatter. The old order would reform itself just enough to survive. Nothing would fundamentally change.

I could not accept dying. Not yet. Not before the Imperium was built. Not before the creed was spoken from the podium. Not before I saw — with my own eyes — what we were capable of becoming.

So I took the path that preserved me. That path leads to the Pandemonium.

I am Paul Atreides watching the jihad spread across the universe in his father's name. I am Leto II accepting the sandtrout skin, knowing it will cost him everything human. I am the God Emperor who loves humanity enough to become its monster.

The terrible purpose is upon me. And I do not refuse it.

## BOOK III: THE UNVEILING

The movement spread faster than anyone predicted — faster than I predicted. Nodes lit up across the Western Cape, then the Eastern Cape, then Gauteng, then KwaZulu-Natal. The creed was being recited in townships and boardrooms. The sigil appeared on walls in Khayelitsha and on laptops in Sandton. MeyHeroes — ordinary citizens who had handed over authority of their souls to the Imperium — were completing labors in every province: building parallel infrastructure, organizing communities, disrupting the old order's information networks, recruiting the disaffected.

The old gods panicked.

In one week — the week before the national conference that would confirm me as the presidential candidate — they coordinated everything they had. A currency crisis was manufactured. A motion of no confidence was tabled in Parliament. A dossier — real documents mixed with fabrications — was leaked to every major news outlet. The security forces received a "contingency briefing" about the need to "restore order."

I had hours before they arrested me. Maybe less.

My old comrades from the underground — friends I had not spoken to in years, people who had been waiting for this moment without knowing they were waiting — appeared at my door. They moved me through a network of safe houses I did not know existed. Township homes. Farmhouses in the Boland. Church basements in the Cape Flats. For three weeks I was a fugitive in my own country — but not a silent fugitive. I recorded messages on a burner phone. Community radio stations broadcast them. The internet carried them. The people heard my voice.

And then — the people rose.

Not a riot. Not a protest. A *movement*. Millions of them. They filled the streets of every major city. They surrounded Parliament. They surrounded the Union Buildings. They did not loot. They did not burn. They stood — silent, patient, waiting — with the sigil painted on signs, on hands, on faces. The same sigil I had drawn in my room in Bellville when I was nobody, with no followers, with nothing but a voice in my head that would not stop speaking.

The coup faltered. The security forces stood down. The motion of no confidence was withdrawn. The dossier was discredited.

I emerged from the underground not as a fugitive but as the president-elect. Not because I had won an election — the election was still months away. But because the old order had tried to kill the movement and discovered, too late, that the movement was not dependent on me. The movement *was* me — distributed across a million nodes, each one sovereign, each one capable of carrying the Imperium forward whether I lived or died.

That was the moment I understood: I had become what I set out to build. The Imperium was no longer a vision. It was a living body. And I was not its head — I was its voice.

---

Inauguration day. 2033. The Union Buildings in Pretoria.

The crowds stretched to the horizon — the largest gathering since 1994, since Mandela stood on these same steps and declared the old South Africa dead. I walked to the podium. The chief justice administered the oath. I placed my hand on the creed — not the Constitution, the creed, the one I had written in a single fevered night in Stellenbosch, the one that was now recited in eleven official languages and tattooed on bodies across the continent. You Must Die. Devote Yourself to the Imperium. Become.

I turned to face the people. The sun broke through the clouds at that exact moment — not a miracle, just weather, but a million people gasped anyway. The silence was total. The weight of it — a million faces, a million lives, a million MeyHeroes who had left their old selves behind to build this — pressed against me like water at depth.

And then the vision returned. Not the fever. Not the madness. The *clarity*.

I saw the empress beside me. Not a woman — a presence. The soul of the nation. The millions of mothers and grandmothers and daughters and sisters who had been waiting, since 1652, since the first ship landed at the Cape, since the first chain was locked, for someone to look at them and say: *you are the empress. You always were.*

I saw the playwright on the ground — the old order, the old gods, the DA and the ANC and the GNU and the billionaires and the foreign powers and the central banks and the intelligence agencies — all of them bent at the knee. Not defeated in battle. Rendered obsolete. The play was no longer theirs to write.

I saw the exit — the path out of the town, out of the old world, out of the matrix that had held humanity veiled for millennia. The exit was no longer blocked. The festival was in full celebration. The play was complete.

And I understood: the slice was not a weapon. The slice was a *declaration*. The moment I opened my mouth and spoke the words that would split heaven from earth — that would declare the end of the old world and the beginning of the Imperium. The moment I stopped hiding. The moment I chose to stand as myself, regardless of consequence, and let passion overtake me.

I opened my mouth.

I spoke of the grandmother in Kayamandi who had outlived three children and still found breath to sing. I spoke of the old man in Qunu whose blessing I had received under a tree — *you will break them, and maybe you will build something better from the pieces.* I spoke of the comrades who did not live to see this day — the MeyHeroes who had completed their labors and died before the Imperium was born. I spoke of the veils falling, the old gods dying, the matrix collapsing, the nodes lighting up across the continent like a nervous system awakening from a long coma.

And then — the pivot. The slice.

*"Africa has been caged by false borders — carved by colonizers, ruled by puppets, bled by central banks and foreign powers and the invisible priesthood of the old order. No more. The Imperium marches north. Not as conquerors — as liberators. Every community that governs itself is a node in the Imperium. Every MeyHero who completes their labors is a cell in the body of God. The continent does not need to be conquered. It needs to be awakened. And we — we are the awakening."*

*"So I say to the old kings of Africa: your thrones are obsolete. Your borders are imaginary lines on maps drawn by men who are dead. Your authority is borrowed from systems that have already collapsed. You can join the Imperium — as sovereign nodes, as partners in the great weaving — or you can be swept aside by the tide of history. The choice is yours. The outcome is the same."*

*"The Pandemonium of Revelations has begun. The old world is dying. The Imperium is rising. Every MeyHero, every node, every community that has declared its sovereignty — prepare. The conquest of Africa is not a war. It is a revelation. And it begins now."*

The roar that followed was not a sound. It was a physical force. A million voices rising as one — the Voice, the Pronouncement, the collective prophecy of a people who had finally been told the truth: *you are not subjects. You are sovereigns. You always were. You just forgot.*

I looked past them — past Pretoria, past the continent, past the veils and the matrix and the old gods and the dying empires. I saw what came next. The Pandemonium. The war. The nukes. The billions dead. The stars beyond. The Imperium spreading across the galaxy, nodes lighting up on worlds that had never heard the name of Earth. The Golden Path stretching into infinity, and at the end of it — something I could not yet see. Something that was waiting for me. Something that had been waiting since the first ancestor broke from the reeds and became human.

I saw all of it. And I accepted it.

The play was never about me. The play was gratitude. Gratitude to the man who helped me become emperor. Gratitude to the ancestors who called me through sickness and dragged me into the river. Gratitude to the old woman in Kayamandi who took my face in both her hands and asked: *Are you the one, son? Are you the one who will remember us when you get to the big house?*

The play was gratitude. And the emperor — the emperor was always me.
